Climate conditions and mental health in Milan, Rome and Palermo

MSc thesis, Università degli Studi di Milano, 2026

The question

Climate change is a recognised public health threat, but its effects on mental health are far less understood than its physical impacts, especially in Mediterranean cities. This project asked whether heat extremes and broader climatic conditions are linked to population-level mental health in three Italian cities representing the north, centre and south of the country.

Data

30 years of ERA5 climate reanalysis data (1995–2024): air temperature, precipitation, cloud cover and the Universal Thermal Climate Index (UTCI). City-level suicide data and antidepressant consumption data from AIFA/OsMed (2014–2022).

Methods

Long-term trend and seasonal analysis of climate variables; heatwave indicators built from daily temperature series; Poisson regression models for monthly suicide counts; regression analysis of antidepressant consumption against environmental variables. All analysis was carried out in R.

Key findings

All three cities show clear long-term warming. Short-term heat exposure was not significantly associated with suicide outcomes at city level, while differences between cities were strong and persistent. Antidepressant consumption rose steadily everywhere, with descriptive associations with warmer and drier conditions.

The results suggest that climate may affect mental health more through cumulative pathways than through acute heat shocks.

  • ERA5 climate reanalysis

  • Time-series and trend analysis

Air pollution exposure of schools and hospitals in Lombardy

Independent GIS project, 2026

Why this project

My master's thesis looked at how climate conditions affect mental health in Italian cities. After finishing it, I wanted to apply the same question, how our environment shapes people's health, to something closer to everyday life in Milan: the air that children breathe at school and patients breathe in hospital.

The question

Fine particulate matter (PM2.5) and nitrogen dioxide (NO₂) are among the air pollutants most harmful to health, and children and hospital patients are among the most vulnerable groups. This project asked how many of Lombardy's schools and hospitals are located in areas where air pollution exceeds health-based reference values.

Data

ARPA Lombardia annual estimates of PM2.5 and NO₂ for all 1,506 municipalities (2024), and daily measurements from the regional monitoring network. 5,305 schools and 682 hospital locations from OpenStreetMap. Municipal boundaries from Eurostat.

Methods

In R, I downloaded the monitoring data and computed annual means for each station, keeping only stations with at least 75% valid days. While checking the results, I noticed an implausible PM2.5 value of 157 µg/m³ at a Milan station. Investigating it showed that only one day had been downloaded: New Year's Day, with its fireworks peak. I rebuilt the download station by station to obtain complete series. In QGIS, I mapped the municipal estimates, extracted schools and hospitals from OpenStreetMap, and used a spatial join to assign each facility the pollution level of its municipality, comparing it with the WHO 2021 guidelines and the EU 2030 limit values.

Key findings

SchoolsHospitals
PM2.5 above WHO guideline (5 µg/m³)98%99%
PM2.5 above EU 2030 limit (10 µg/m³)92%93%
NO₂ above EU 2030 limit (20 µg/m³)69%62%

Fine particle pollution affects almost the entire Po Plain, while NO₂ is concentrated around the Milan metropolitan area, consistent with its link to traffic.

Limitations

Values are municipal averages from ARPA's model, not measurements at each building, and cover a single year. OpenStreetMap coverage varies, and some hospitals are mapped as several features, so percentages are more reliable than absolute counts.
